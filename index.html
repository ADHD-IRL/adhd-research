An independent research initiative on adult cognition

Assess how a mind engages,
not just which label fits.

Adult ADHD is poorly understood, and the label may not even name the real challenge. Trouble starting tasks, holding effort, and sensing time also shows up with anxiety, depression, autism, poor sleep, and hormonal change. This initiative started with ADHD and now aims further: a cognitive profile of attention, motivation, and time that works across diagnoses, built for the more dimensional diagnostic manual now being planned.

Why look beyond ADHD Skip to what helps today
  • Labels overlap. Most adults with ADHD meet criteria for at least one other condition.
  • The real differences are dimensions, like how much effort feels worth it, or how fast future rewards fade.
  • Context changes everything. The same person can focus for hours or not for minutes, depending on the task.

The reframe

Why look beyond ADHD

A diagnosis answers "which box?" It says little about why a particular person stalls on a particular task. The evidence increasingly points to shared dimensions that cut across diagnoses.

Overlap is the norm

Up to 80% of adults with ADHD have at least one other condition. They are about five times as likely to have anxiety and four to five times as likely to have depression. In a clinic, "pure" adult ADHD is the exception.

The traits are continuous

Statistical studies in children and adults find that ADHD traits form a spectrum with no natural cut-off. Any diagnosis draws a line on a continuum, and scores along it predict outcomes better than yes-or-no.

The same problems cross labels

Reduced willingness to work for rewards appears in depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia. Hyperfocus is raised in both autism and ADHD. Late body clocks and poor sleep run through many conditions.

Markers don't respect the boxes

Nearly every proposed ADHD biomarker fails to separate ADHD from anxiety or depression. That is a weakness for diagnosis, but it suggests these measures track something real that the categories split apart.

Newer maps group conditions differently

Genetic studies across 11 disorders found a shared neurodevelopmental factor. The HiTOP framework (2024) groups ADHD with autism and learning differences on a neurodevelopmental spectrum, measured as dimensions.

The manual itself is changing

In January 2026 the American Psychiatric Association published a roadmap for the next DSM. It proposes an online "living" manual that adds dimensional, contextual, and biological information alongside diagnoses. No release date has been set.

A profile, not just a yes or no

The goal is an assessment that describes how a person's attention and motivation actually work, whatever their diagnosis. Each dimension below comes from the research already on this site and can be measured within one person across different situations.

Engagement by context

How much interest, novelty, urgency, and other people change focus on the same task.

Starting versus sustaining

Whether the problem is getting going, keeping going, or doing the work well once started.

Effort and reward

Whether effort feels unusually costly, rewards feel unusually small, or both. This is where ADHD and depression may differ.

Time sense

How fast future rewards lose their pull, and how strongly deadlines rescue performance.

Arousal, sleep, and body clock

When in the day the mind works best, and how sleep and alertness shape everything else.

Emotion and contributing factors

Emotional regulation, plus mood, anxiety, hormones, thyroid, and iron, which add to or mimic attention problems.

This profile is a research goal, not a test anyone can take yet. Each measure must first prove it gives the same answer twice and predicts real-life outcomes. Diagnoses like ADHD stay in place for treatment and support; the profile would sit alongside them.

Current evidence

What ADHD research shows

ADHD is one of the most studied conditions in psychiatry, which makes it the best starting point. Its findings also show where the label runs out.

Attention depends on the task

Engagement rises sharply with interest, novelty, challenge, urgency, and immediate reward. Intense focus on high-interest tasks ("hyperfocus") and difficulty with low-interest tasks are two sides of the same pattern. Rewards help, but they rarely close the gap completely.

Dopamine plays a role

Brain imaging studies show differences in the dopamine reward pathway, linked to motivation and attention symptoms. Stimulant medication measurably makes a dull task feel more interesting.

No single test exists

No brain scan, blood test, or EEG reading can diagnose ADHD in an individual. A once FDA-cleared EEG measure held up in almost none of 576 reanalyses in 2026. Diagnosis still relies on clinical assessment of history and daily impact.

Many things produce the same symptoms

Poor sleep, sleep apnea, anxiety, depression, trauma, thyroid problems, low iron, hormonal changes, and learning differences can all produce ADHD-like symptoms, or make them worse. More often they add to ADHD than replace it, which is why a profile of contributors matters more than a single label.

Time works differently

Many people with ADHD describe "time-blindness": not feeling the pull of future deadlines until they are close. The clearest evidence is that distant rewards lose value faster. Lab timing tests show only moderate differences, mostly over fractions of a second.

It is not about effort or character

ADHD is highly heritable and reflects differences in how the brain regulates attention and motivation. Willpower-based advice tends to fail; changing the task and environment works better, with or without a diagnosis.

The core question

Why one task and not another?

The brain constantly weighs whether a task is worth the effort. A useful summary comes from Temporal Motivation Theory:

Motivation ≈ Expectancy × Value Impulsiveness × Delay

For many people, including most with ADHD, the bottom half of that equation does most of the damage. In others, such as many people with depression, the top half is the bigger problem. Telling them apart is part of the profile.

Distant rewards fade fast

A reward next month feels worth almost nothing today. "This matters later" barely registers as motivation now.

The "it'll pay off" signal is weak

The brain's anticipatory dopamine signal for future payoff is blunted, so long-term goals don't pull hard enough.

Interesting tasks pay out while you do them

Games, hobbies, and puzzles reward every second through novelty, feedback, and mastery. Dull tasks pay only at the end.

Urgency switches the brain on

Deadlines and pressure raise arousal into the working range, which is why so much gets done at the last minute.

Why prioritizing is so hard

Now

Reply to that message Interesting rabbit hole Deadline in 2 hours

Not now

Tax return (due in 6 weeks) Doctor appointment Career plan Laundry

Comparing tasks means holding several in mind at once, imagining their future consequences, and weighing later against now. Those are exactly the systems that ADHD, poor sleep, anxiety, and low mood all strain. So priority gets set by what is most immediate and salient, not what is most important. Everything in "not now" feels equally unimportant. Too many similar options causes paralysis, and avoiding a dreaded task brings relief that makes avoiding it again more likely.

The research approach

A staged plan to build a better adult assessment

The program uses adult ADHD as its starting point because it has the deepest research base. It starts as independent concept work and low-risk pilots, then moves into an academic setting for clinical, imaging, and intervention studies across diagnoses.

  1. Phase A · Independent

    Frame the questions

    Evidence review, concept papers, open-source task tools, and small ethics-reviewed online pilots: surveys, phone check-ins, and remote experiments.

  2. Transition

    Find academic partners

    Collaborate with university groups in ADHD, mood and anxiety, sleep, imaging, and digital health. Use pilot data to apply for research funding.

  3. Phase B · Academic

    Test it properly

    Clinical cohorts, lab and brain-imaging studies, and controlled trials of interventions, all under institutional oversight.

Five research streams, one core question

Reviews of current research found that the most promising, least explored question is how a person's engagement changes with the conditions around a task, in any diagnosis. That question sits at the center. The other streams feed it, and a fifth turns the results into proposals for how adult conditions are named and classified.

1

What sits behind the symptoms

Screen adults with attention problems for sleep, mood, medical, hormonal, sensory, and learning factors. Treat what's found and re-measure. How much of what gets called "ADHD" is something else, or several things at once, and treatable?

2

Measures that track engagement

Reaction-time patterns, pupil size, mind-wandering checks, and sleep data, used to track how a person's attention changes across situations and treatments. Decades of searching have not produced a diagnostic biomarker for ADHD, so this stream measures dimensions instead of trying to detect a label.

3

What captures attention (the core)

Vary reward, novelty, urgency, interest, social presence, and the size of the first step on the same task, in the lab and in daily life, in adults with and without a diagnosis. Find what works, for whom, and whether it lasts. Later, use brain imaging to examine the dopamine response.

4

Interventions that work

Low-cost personal trials and in-the-moment app prompts come first. Device and medication trials follow only with academic partners and the right oversight. Success means better daily functioning that lasts after support is removed.

5

From labels to profiles

"Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder" calls up a restless child, and it says nothing about how an adult's mind actually works. Test clearer names, and propose profile dimensions that the next DSM could record alongside diagnoses, without changing anyone's access to care. More below.

New ways to measure engagement and time

Most tests measure how well someone does one task. This approach measures the gap between versions of the same task within one person:

Interest Gradient

Performance on a task with dull content versus content from the person's own interests. A large gap marks interest-dependent attention, common in ADHD but not limited to it.

Urgency Rescue

How sharply output improves as a deadline approaches. A steep late surge points to steep discounting of the future, sometimes called time-blindness.

Starting Delay

Time before starting, measured separately from performance once working. It separates "can't start" from "can't do."

These measures are new and unproven. Gaps between two scores are often less consistent than the scores themselves, so the first study checks whether each measure gives the same answer twice, and publishes the result either way.

More detection ideas: everyday data, eyes, and EEG
  • Deadline curves from real life: timestamps on submissions, document edits, or replies show how steeply someone discounts the future.
  • Effort tasks: short games that ask how much effort someone will put in for different rewards. They can show whether effort feels costlier, rewards feel smaller, or delays matter more, and they borrow methods from depression research.
  • Eyes (exploratory): pupil size tracks effort and arousal, and holding the eyes still before an expected event is a candidate measure of time-blindness. Blink timing may track engagement and long, slow blinks flag drowsiness. Blink rate is not used as a dopamine signal, because brain-scan studies in healthy adults found no link.
  • Low-cost EEG headbands (exploratory): limited to brain responses timed to events in a task, compared within one person across conditions. They cannot diagnose any condition or measure dopamine.
  • Virtual-reality errand tasks: a simulated office with competing jobs measures planning and prioritization directly.
  • Body signals at the moment of starting: pupil size, heart rate, and skin response when a dreaded task appears.

Proposed experiment

The Engagement Transplant Study

Can the focus someone brings to their favorite activity be transplanted onto a task they avoid?

  1. Profile. Each participant names an activity they hyperfocus on and a task they avoid, and breaks the activity down into its ingredients.
  2. Graft in the lab. Planned combinations of ingredients (immediate reward, urgency, novelty, social presence, a smaller first step) are added to one common task with personal content, plus a plain version for comparison.
  3. Graft in daily life. For four to six weeks, an app randomly offers an ingredient, or nothing, each time the person sits down to a real task, and logs how fast they start and how long they keep going.
  4. Measure. Getting started and staying on task are measured separately from accuracy once working, along with pupil signals, mind-wandering, boredom, and how it felt.
  5. Check it lasts. Each person's best ingredients are removed and reintroduced to see how much of the gain remains.

Who takes part: adults with ADHD, with anxiety or depression, with more than one of these, and with none. Comparing them shows which engagement patterns belong to a diagnosis and which cut across all of them, which is the evidence a dimensional manual would need.

Outcomes: a personal "engagement recipe" for each person, evidence on which ingredients work best, for whom and for how long, and rules for an app that offers the right prompt at the right moment. It can start as a low-cost remote pilot. No published study has yet tested several engagement ingredients together within the same people, in ADHD or across diagnoses; that still needs a formal literature search to confirm.

Stream 5

From labels to profiles: naming and classification

When people imagine adult ADHD, they tend to picture a hyperactive child grown up. In one study, adults asked to fake adult ADHD overplayed hyperactivity and missed the self-esteem and emotional struggles that are common in real adults with ADHD. Disbelief takes two forms: "that's a childhood thing" and "everyone's a bit like that."

What the evidence says so far:

  • It's one condition, not two. Most apparent cases that start in adulthood turn out, on careful repeated assessment, to have other explanations. ADHD in adults is the same condition as in children, though it looks different: hyperactivity often becomes inner restlessness, and emotional swings are common.
  • A brand-new adult diagnosis would be risky. Insurance codes, medication approvals, and workplace and school accommodations are tied to the ADHD diagnosis. A separate adult category could disrupt all three.
  • Renaming helps, but modestly. When Japan renamed schizophrenia and dementia, more people were willing to share their diagnosis, mostly when the new name came with a new explanation. A name chosen without the people it describes, like the proposed "SEID" for chronic fatigue syndrome, was rejected.
  • The next manual is moving toward dimensions. The APA's 2026 roadmap for the next DSM adds dimensional and contextual layers alongside diagnoses. A validated profile of engagement, effort, and time would fit that direction better than a new adult category.

The approach: keep ADHD as the medical diagnosis so care and support are not disrupted. Add descriptions that fit adults (such as "with prominent emotional dysregulation"), and develop a clearer public name defined as "also known as ADHD." Possible names will be tested in online experiments and chosen with adults who have ADHD. In parallel, once the profile dimensions from streams 2 and 3 prove reliable, they would be proposed as a cross-diagnosis layer. Proposals go to the bodies that maintain the diagnostic manuals (the American Psychiatric Association's DSM and the WHO's ICD).

No alternative name for ADHD has ever been tested experimentally. Candidates under consideration, such as "Attention and Self-Regulation Condition," are early ideas, not recommendations. One aim is to find out whether people with ADHD want a new name at all.

Borrowing from neighbouring fields

Other areas of research have tested tools that have barely been tried in ADHD, and many already work across diagnoses:

Depression and schizophrenia research

Effort tasks that separate "the reward feels small" from "the effort feels big." A 2026 review across conditions found the pattern differs by diagnosis, so these tasks could show how motivation fails for each person.

Addiction treatment

Rewards that grow with each success and reset after a miss work well for addiction (74 trials), but gains can vanish when rewards stop. They have never been tested for everyday tasks in ADHD.

Behavior-change science

A 61,000-person gym study tested 54 ideas. About half worked while running, only 8% kept working afterwards, and the best one rewarded coming back after a missed session.

Mobile health

Trials that randomize app prompts many times a day show which prompt helps which person, in which moment. None has been run in ADHD.

Attention-state science

Quick checks for mind-wandering, boredom, curiosity, and hyperfocus capture what a person's attention is doing at that moment.

Sleep and body clock

Most adults with ADHD have a late body clock, so the time of day can change results. Every session records it.

Popular findings this program won't rely on
  • Self-set deadlines boost performance: a famous 2002 study failed a careful replication in 2026, and its data are now under question. The program tests real external deadlines instead.
  • Unfinished tasks stick in memory (the Zeigarnik effect): a 2025 review found the memory effect doesn't hold, though people do tend to return to unfinished tasks.
  • Small nudges reliably change behavior: after correcting for unpublished null results, the average effect is close to zero, though some nudges work well for some people.
  • Body doubling works: it may, but no controlled study has tested it yet. That is one of the gaps this program aims to fill.

Practical

What helps today

The same principle that drives the research works in daily life, with or without a diagnosis: add what the boring task is missing. Most of the evidence comes from ADHD studies. Evidence levels are shown so you can judge for yourself.

Strong evidence

If you have ADHD, ask about medication

Stimulants measurably increase how rewarding a task feels. For diagnosed ADHD they are among the most effective treatments studied in psychiatry.

Good evidence, not yet in adult ADHD

Make starting tiny

Commit only to the first step ("open the document"). Set things up the night before. Use "when X happens, I do Y" plans, which work in the general population but haven't been tested in adults with ADHD.

Moderate

Create urgency with someone else

Work in 15–25 minute timeboxes. Use deadlines someone else sets or checks, like telling a person when you'll be done. Deadlines you set only for yourself have tested poorly.

Moderate

Move first

A short burst of exercise before a demanding task gives a small-to-moderate boost to focus and self-control.

Modest; fades

Bundle the task with a treat

Save a favorite podcast, playlist, or drink for only while you do the dull task. Effects are smaller in larger studies and fade, so swap the treat now and then.

Modest

Reward the comeback

After a missed day, give yourself a small reward for getting back on track. It was the best of 54 strategies in a large gym study.

Small, unblinded studies

Try white or pink noise

Background noise gave a small benefit in ADHD and a small cost for people without ADHD. Studies so far weren't blinded.

Plausible

Make time visible

Use a visual timer, see time as space on a calendar, or set gentle vibration reminders.

Popular, untested

Body doubling

Work alongside another person, in the room or on video, even if they're doing something else. Many people find it helps, but it hasn't been tested in a controlled study.

Plausible

Keep it fresh

Rotate where and how you work. Race your last time. Novelty fades, so keep rotating.

Plausible

Show one next task

Hide the full list. Let a person, a rule, or an app choose the single next thing so you don't have to compare.

Supplements

Supplements are popular, but most have small or inconsistent effects on ADHD and none replaces evidence-based treatment. Several help mainly when a person is actually deficient, so a blood test is the sensible first step. The same evidence badges apply.

Early, not tested in ADHD

Zinc glycinate for sleep

Zinc plays a role in sleep regulation, and most of a handful of small trials outside ADHD found zinc improved adults' self-rated sleep quality, though not diagnosed sleep disorders. No trial has tested it for sleep in ADHD. One small study found the glycinate form better absorbed than zinc gluconate. Keep total zinc, from food and supplements, under 40 mg a day; more, long term, can cause copper deficiency.

Only if deficient; very limited evidence

Iron

Children with ADHD have lower iron stores (ferritin) on average. The only treatment trial was a small pilot in 23 children with low ferritin, with mixed results, and no adult trial exists. There is no reason to take it if levels are normal. Get tested first, and keep iron tablets away from young children, as overdose is dangerous.

Little or no benefit

Omega-3 fish oil

The largest review (Cochrane, 2023, 37 trials) found no effect on ADHD symptoms, and UK guidelines (NICE) advise against it for ADHD. Some analyses hint at a small benefit in longer trials or in children with low omega-3 levels, but these are unconfirmed.

Small, add-on only

Vitamin D

Four small trials in children taking stimulant medication found a small extra benefit from adding vitamin D, with low-certainty evidence. There are no adult trials. Correcting a deficiency is worthwhile for general health anyway.

Small, mixed

Broad micronutrient formulas

In a well-run 8-week trial in unmedicated children with ADHD and irritability, more children on micronutrients were rated much improved overall (54% versus 18%), but parent-rated ADHD symptoms did not improve more than placebo. One adult trial of a commercial formula found self-rated but not clinician-rated improvement.

Small, low-quality studies

Zinc and magnesium for focus

Six small trials in children found zinc improved overall ADHD scores, but not attention or hyperactivity on their own, with low-certainty evidence. Magnesium levels are slightly lower in ADHD, but it has mostly been tested in combination products.

Small, low-quality studies

Saffron

A few small trials in children, mostly from one country, compared it with stimulant medication in one trial and added it to medication in others. No trial has tested saffron alone against placebo, and results need independent replication.

Not shown to help ADHD

Caffeine

It briefly increases alertness, but pooled trials in children with ADHD found no clear benefit over placebo, and medication worked better in a head-to-head trial. It can worsen sleep and anxiety and adds to the stimulant effects of ADHD medication.

Tested; no clear benefit

St John's wort and ginkgo

St John's wort did no better than placebo in a controlled trial in children, and it interacts with many medicines, including antidepressants and birth control pills. Ginkgo worked far less well than medication in one trial and gave a modest, unreplicated boost when added to it in another.

Supplements are not reviewed for effectiveness before sale, and quality varies between brands. Some interact with ADHD medication or other drugs. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before starting one, especially when pregnant or taking other medication.

Also worth checking: sleep and body-clock timing, mood, anxiety, thyroid, iron levels, and for women, menstrual-cycle and perimenopause changes. Treating these can make a large difference, whatever the diagnosis.

How this work is done

Principles

Outcomes people choose

The goal is better daily life by each person's own measure, such as tasks finished, goals met, and less stress. It is not about making anyone "normal."

Profiles alongside labels

Describe how a person's attention and motivation work, not only which diagnosis fits. Diagnoses stay in place so no one loses access to care.

Designed with lived experience

People with ADHD and related conditions help shape the questions, tasks, and measures.

Look at every contributor

Check sleep, mood, hormones, and medical factors before attributing anything to a single condition.

Open and careful science

Ethics review for every study involving people, plans registered before data is collected, and higher-risk work only with the right oversight.

Gains that last

A strategy counts as working only if the benefit holds up after the extra support is taken away.

Only findings that hold up

Claims are checked against replications before being used, and null results are published too.
